# C1

Have you heard of the phrase "once you finish your PhD, your academic clock starts ticking"? What do you think about it?

Here is the real academic answer to this question that you'll probably hear less often: the academic job is a pretty nice job. You get paid pretty well -- not as well as other jobs you might get with a PhD, but probably better than an average artist, for example; you get to do math all day, meet with students, teach, and paid to travel. It's a pretty cushy job. Unfortunately (or fortunately), there are more people who want this job than there are job openings. So, by the Pigeonhole Principle, there's a lot of competition. If there were less people than job openings, then the academic clock wouldn't matter. But even though there's a lot of stress and competition, you shouldn't forget that 1) you are doing it for a really good job, and 2) it's just a job -- so if it is making you miserable, then you can quit. A lot of people tie their sense of identity to being a mathematician -- this really cool thing of doing math for your whole life -- and I'm certainly not immune to this myself, but the healthy perspective is to remember that this is just a job. So there is no harm in seeing where your life takes you, and making lots of different decisions. It will all be OK.


Best tip for Stanford?

The difference between studying for one qual and studying for two quals is huge. Attempt one qualifying exam upon entry, and take it seriously. Study very hard for two months over the summer, and there's a very good chance you'll pass. There were lots of people who passed both upon entry -- great for them. There were also some people who didn't attempt any qual on the first try, and just passed them on the second try. These people were also successful -- but they were a lot more stressed. You don't lose anything by at least attempting the quals upon entry, and you lose a freebie shot if you don't. Failing an exam is unpleasant, but there's no harm in failing, and there's a chance you'll pass.


Second best tip for Stanford?

The basement offices are the best. They have the windows with the most sunlight and no tourists are walking outside your door, removing potentially awkward encounters.


Third best tip for Stanford?

At any point after you pass your quals, you can apply for a master's degree. It's not as important in the US, but it can matter if you want to come back to Europe. For example, German universities count the total number of degrees you have when considering your application, so you'd need a master's. You might need to write a short thesis, but this shouldn't be a problem in the end.


Let's have one more. Fourth best tip for Stanford?

Your working relationship with your advisor is partially determined by you. Some advisors meet their students more, and some less. If you'd prefer to meet once a week, then you should make this clear with your advisor. You should also meet with your advisor even if you have nothing, and it's just for ten minutes. It's really about giving yourself a deadline.


Have you ever taken a prolonged break from math, and had to recover and "dive back into it"?

Personally, I haven't. But I know a handful of people who have -- including breaks of 1, 3, and 7 years -- and they were able to do it. It's hard, but doable, so don't feel like you're doing something new.

In my personal experience, I went into Stanford as a topologist. When I was visiting, there was a large topology group with great people. But within the time between when I visited and when I started, many of those people left or were unavailable, through a variety of circumstances. That meant I had some time to do some reading courses and try to learn some other math. I took lots of courses, and eventually I took one with a professor who I ended up asking to be my advisor. I really enjoyed reading with him (he's a bit shy), and so I ended up switching to number theory during my second year. This was quite stressful, because I had to catch up on a lot of background that other students in my year seemed to already have. I doubted whether I would get a PhD or not at that point. It was helpful to know that there was this safety net of exiting with a master's degree, though this was never my intention. By the time I was in my fourth and fifth year, I had internalized that, yes, I was getting my PhD. But at that point, I was stressed about applying for jobs -- and I had a two body problem, which was more difficult. So there's always something new to stress about, and the things that you were already stressed about that had worked out already become less important. It's healthy to take a step back and realize that, in this job, there will constantly be things to stressed about. That is one way to motivate yourself, but you shouldn't let it consume you.
